Pesticide Residues on Basmati and Spices: What to Ask Your Supplier

What residue reports to ask an Indian basmati or spice supplier for: lab accreditation, reporting limits, EU tricyclazole at 0.01 mg/kg, EtO, and Gulf rules.

Rice21 May 20245 min readDELxPort

This guide tells you what residue documents to ask an Indian supplier of basmati or spices for, how to check that the lab and the report are fit for your market, and where EU and Gulf rules differ. It reflects the rules as they stand in May 2024.

Why residues decide whether a container clears

Every importing country sets maximum residue levels (MRLs) for pesticides in food. A lot that exceeds the limit at the border can be detained, re-exported or destroyed, and in the EU it is published on the RASFF alert system. One failed container can also put a supplier or an origin under tighter checks for all buyers.

Residue rules vary more between markets than most quality specs. A lot that is legal in India or the Gulf may fail in the EU.

The EU approach

The EU sets MRLs under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. Two features catch exporters out:

  • Default limit. Where no specific MRL exists for a substance on a crop, a default of 0.01 mg/kg applies. That is close to the limit of what labs can measure.
  • Substances not approved in the EU. When an active substance is not approved for use in the EU, its MRL is often set at the limit of quantification. Imports must meet it even if the substance is legal where the crop was grown.

Tricyclazole on basmati

Tricyclazole is a fungicide widely used in India against rice blast. Since 2017 the EU MRL for tricyclazole in rice has been 0.01 mg/kg, down from 1 mg/kg, with a short transition for basmati that ended around the start of 2018. It is the substance most often behind EU alerts on Indian rice.

In 2023 the European Commission proposed raising the limit to 0.09 mg/kg on the basis of an import tolerance assessed by EFSA. On 14 December 2023 the European Parliament voted to oppose the proposal and insisted the limit for imported rice stay at 0.01 mg/kg. As of May 2024 the 0.01 mg/kg limit still applies.

In practice, basmati for the EU needs:

  • Paddy bought from farmers in residue-control programmes, or tested before purchase
  • Segregation of tested paddy and rice through milling and storage
  • A lot-specific residue report with a reporting limit at or below 0.01 mg/kg for tricyclazole

UK

Great Britain sets its own MRLs after Brexit. Many values still match those the EU had before 2021, but they can now diverge. Check the GB MRL database for each substance rather than assuming the EU figure.

Spices: more than pesticides

Spices face pesticide MRLs plus other contaminant checks.

  • Ethylene oxide (EtO). EtO is not approved for use on food in the EU. Since 2020 the EU has acted on EtO residues found in products from India. In April 2024 food regulators in Hong Kong and Singapore recalled some Indian spice blends over EtO. In May 2024 the Spices Board issued guidelines telling exporters to keep EtO out of the supply chain, test raw materials and finished goods, and use alternatives such as steam sterilisation.
  • Chilli to the UK. From 7 March 2024 the UK increased checks on chilli from India. The Spices Board now requires pre-export pesticide residue testing and an official certificate for chilli shipped to the UK, on top of existing aflatoxin testing.
  • Aflatoxin and illegal dyes. Chilli, nutmeg and some other spices are also tested for aflatoxin and for Sudan dyes. Ask for these alongside the pesticide report.

The Gulf approach

GCC states apply pesticide residue standards issued through the GCC Standardization Organization (GSO), with national authorities such as the Saudi Food and Drug Authority enforcing them. Limits for many substance and crop pairs differ from EU values, and for some substances the EU has not approved they can be higher. Basmati that would fail in the EU on tricyclazole may be acceptable in a Gulf market, depending on the national limit.

That does not make testing optional. Gulf authorities do sample and reject consignments, and private-label buyers increasingly set their own residue specs. Check the current limit for the substances that matter in your product with your local authority or a lab that knows the destination.

What to ask your supplier for

1. A residue report on your lot

A generic annual certificate is not enough. The report should carry the lot or batch number, the sampling date and who drew the sample. For the EU and UK, ask for testing on the actual export lot after final processing.

2. A properly accredited lab

  • The lab should be accredited to ISO/IEC 17025. In India the national accreditation body is NABL.
  • Check the scope of accreditation. It should cover the analytes you care about in the relevant food matrix. A lab accredited for pesticides in vegetables is not automatically accredited for pesticides in chilli powder.
  • For basmati, ask whether the lab is recognised by APEDA. For spices, the Spices Board runs its own quality evaluation labs, which handle the mandatory pre-export tests for some markets.

3. The right method and reporting limits

  • Multi-residue screens by LC-MS/MS and GC-MS/MS cover several hundred pesticides in one run.
  • Some substances need separate single-residue methods. Ask specifically about dithiocarbamates, glyphosate and, for spices, ethylene oxide (reported as the sum of EtO and 2-chloroethanol).
  • The reporting limit must be at or below the destination MRL. A report showing "not detected" at 0.05 mg/kg tells you nothing about a 0.01 mg/kg limit.

4. Results laid out against your limits

Ask for each detected residue with its value next to the destination MRL. Results reported as "complies" without numbers are hard to audit.

5. Sampling you can trust

For larger orders, appoint an independent surveyor to draw samples at stuffing and seal them. Keep a counter sample so you can retest if a border lab disagrees.

Before you order

  • Name the destination market so the supplier tests against the right limits
  • Ask for a lot-specific residue report from an ISO/IEC 17025 (NABL) lab with the analytes in scope
  • Confirm reporting limits at or below destination MRLs, including 0.01 mg/kg where it applies
  • For EU basmati, check tricyclazole specifically
  • For spices, add EtO, aflatoxin and Sudan dye tests where relevant
  • Agree independent sampling and keep a counter sample
