Private-label spices and masalas: grinding, packaging and the spec

Whole vs ground spices, particle size and grinding heat, barrier films, moisture and shelf life, and what a private-label spice and masala spec should include.

Private label3 Jun 20255 min readDELxPort

This guide will help you decide between whole and ground spices for a private-label range, set particle size and packaging, and write a spec your supplier can be held to.

Whole or ground

Most ranges carry both. Each has trade-offs for a private-label buyer.

Whole Ground
Flavour life Long; volatile oil sits inside the seed or bark Shorter; grinding exposes oil to air
Microbial risk Lower surface exposure Higher; often needs steam treatment
Adulteration risk Easier to see foreign seeds or dyes Harder to detect without lab tests
Customer convenience Lower Higher
Packing Tolerates lower-barrier packs Needs better barrier against moisture, light and aroma loss

A practical approach: sell high-volume cooking spices (chilli, turmeric, coriander, cumin powder) and masalas ground, and keep whole spices for the cooks who temper and grind at home.

Grinding and particle size

Fix the particle size in numbers

"Fine powder" means different things to different millers. Write it as a sieve result, for example "minimum 95% passing through a 500 micron sieve" or a stated mesh size. Common ranges:

  • Coarse (crushed chilli, coarse black pepper): visible pieces, often 1–3 mm
  • Medium (most cooking powders, many masalas): roughly 30–60 mesh
  • Fine (turmeric, some chilli powders for colour): 60 mesh and finer

Finer powders give better colour release and a smoother texture, but lose aroma faster and cake more easily in humid markets. Agree the range with your test kitchen before production.

Control heat during grinding

Hammer and pin mills generate heat, and heat drives off volatile oil. Options in rising order of cost:

  • Ambient grinding with controlled feed rate and good airflow
  • Water-cooled grinders
  • Cryogenic grinding with liquid nitrogen, which keeps more volatile oil in high-value spices such as cardamom, cumin and black pepper

Ask your supplier which method they use and request volatile oil results on the ground product, not only on the whole raw material.

Masalas

  • Get the formula in writing, with each ingredient and its percentage. You own the recipe if you paid for its development; say so in the contract.
  • Agree whether ingredients are roasted, and to what colour.
  • Specify the blending time and a uniformity check, since heavy and light particles separate in transit.
  • The ingredients list must follow your market's rules, usually in descending order by weight.

Packaging films and barrier

Ground spices need protection from three things: moisture, oxygen and light. Aroma also escapes through some films. Two numbers describe a film's barrier:

  • WVTR (water vapour transmission rate), in g/m²/day
  • OTR (oxygen transmission rate), in cc/m²/day

Lower is better for both. Ask the converter for the figures on the laminate you choose.

Laminate (outside to inside) Barrier Notes
PET / PE Low to medium Clear; fine for fast-moving whole spices
BOPP / CPP Low to medium Low cost; limited aroma barrier
PET / MET-PET / PE Good Metallised layer blocks light; common for ground spices
PET / aluminium foil / PE Very high Best for long shelf life, export and humid markets
Kraft paper with barrier liner Depends on liner Premium look; check the liner

Points to watch:

  • Windows let light in. Chilli and turmeric fade under shop lights.
  • Plain PE in contact with the spice can absorb some aroma.
  • Zippers help after opening but add cost and a weak point for seal failures.
  • Jars and shakers (PET or glass) need a sealed inner liner or induction seal.
  • Seal strength and leak tests should be part of the converter's specification.

Moisture and shelf life

Moisture is the main driver of caking, mould and loss of quality. The European Spice Association recommends a maximum water activity of 0.65 for dried spices. Moisture limits in specs commonly sit around 8–12%, depending on the spice.

Typical best-before periods used by spice packers, assuming good barrier packaging and dry storage:

Product Typical best-before
Whole spices, high-barrier pack 18–24 months
Ground spices, metallised or foil laminate 12–18 months
Masalas with oil-rich ingredients 9–12 months
Ground spices in clear, low-barrier packs 6–9 months

Set your date after a shelf-life check: hold packed samples at normal and raised temperature and humidity, then test aroma, colour and moisture at intervals.

What to put in the spec

A good spice spec covers identity, physical quality, chemistry, safety and packing.

Identity and form - Common and botanical name, origin, whole or ground, particle size

Physical and chemical - Moisture, max % - Total ash and acid-insoluble ash, max % - Volatile oil, min ml/100 g - Extraneous matter, max % - Colour: ASTA colour for chilli and paprika; curcumin % for turmeric - Pungency for chilli (SHU or capsaicinoids)

Food safety - Microbiology: total plate count, yeasts and moulds, E. coli, Salmonella absent in 25 g - Sterilisation method (for example steam) and a statement that ethylene oxide is not used - Pesticide residues to destination MRLs - Mycotoxins where relevant. In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2023/915 sets maximum levels including ochratoxin A of 20 µg/kg for capsicum spices and 15 µg/kg for most other spices and spice mixes, and aflatoxin limits for chilli, pepper, nutmeg, ginger and turmeric - Heavy metals, including lead, against destination limits - Absence of illegal dyes such as Sudan dyes - Allergens: in the EU and UK, mustard and celery are regulated allergens. Masalas that contain them must declare them, and lines that also process them need a cross-contact review

Packing and labelling - Pack type, laminate structure, net weight and tolerance - Lot coding format, best-before date, carton marks - Pallet or carton configuration for export

Documents per lot - Certificate of analysis tied to the lot number - Sterilisation record, if treated - Phytosanitary certificate, and fumigation certificate for sea cargo where applicable

Before you order

  • Whole or ground decided per line, with particle size as a sieve result
  • Grinding method and volatile oil limits agreed
  • Masala formulas documented and owned
  • Laminate chosen, with WVTR and OTR from the converter
  • Best-before date backed by a shelf-life check
  • Full spec, including safety limits for your destination, signed by both sides
