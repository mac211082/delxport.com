Planning a mixed container for a resort pantry

How resort buyers build a pantry item list, choose kitchen pack sizes, balance weight against volume in a mixed container and set a re-order cycle.

Hotels28 Jan 20255 min readDELxPort

This guide will help you build a resort pantry order that fills a container sensibly, with pack sizes your kitchens can use and a re-order rhythm that fits island shipping.

Start with what the kitchens actually use

A good container order starts with a consumption record. Pull at least six months of issues from the stores system, ideally twelve, so you see both high and low season. For each item note:

  • average weekly usage, in kg or litres
  • peak weekly usage (festive weeks, full occupancy, special menus)
  • how many weeks of stock you can physically store
  • how long the item keeps in your storeroom

From this you get a par level per item. Without it, the order tends to repeat last time's list and the storeroom ends up long on some lines and short on others.

Building the item list

Group the list the way a supplier packs it. It makes the quote, the packing list and the load plan easier to check.

  • Rice: basmati (raw or sella, depending on the buffet), a non-basmati table rice such as sona masoori or ponni for staff meals, broken rice for idli and dosa batter if your kitchen makes it.
  • Flours and grains: atta (whole wheat), maida, besan, semolina (suji/rava), rice flour, poha, vermicelli.
  • Pulses: toor, moong, masoor, urad, chana dal, kabuli chana, rajma.
  • Oils and fats: refined sunflower or rice bran oil for frying, mustard oil, ghee.
  • Spices: whole (cumin, coriander, mustard seed, cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, dried red chilli) and ground (turmeric, chilli, coriander, garam masala).
  • Dry goods: sugar, salt, tea, jaggery, papad, pickles, dry fruits and nuts.

Keep chef "nice to have" items on a separate tab. Add them only after the core lines are covered.

Pack sizes for resort kitchens

On islands, goods are often moved by hand from boat to jetty to storeroom. Packs of 25 kg or less are easier and safer to handle. Smaller packs also mean fewer opened bags sitting in a humid store.

Item Common kitchen pack Why
Basmati rice 5, 10 or 20 kg bags 5–10 kg suits small outlets; 20 kg for the main kitchen
Staff-meal rice 25 kg bags Lowest cost per kg, high turnover
Atta, maida, besan 10 or 25 kg sacks Flour has a shorter life, so do not overbuy large sacks
Pulses 25 or 30 kg bags Fast-moving, keeps well when dry
Cooking oil 15 L tins or 15 kg jars; 5 L jerry cans for outlets Tins stack and resist knocks
Ghee 1 L, 5 L or 15 kg tins Size by outlet
Whole spices 1 kg or 5 kg laminated pouches Reseal after opening
Ground spices 500 g or 1 kg pouches Smaller packs keep aroma
Sugar, salt 25 or 50 kg bags Bulk staples

Ask for best-before dates on every pack and on the packing list. It saves time at receiving.

Balancing weight and volume

A container has two limits: weight and space. Dense goods such as rice, sugar, pulses and oil use up weight quickly. Light goods such as poha, papad, vermicelli and whole dried chilli use up space quickly. A mixed pantry box usually runs out of space before it runs out of weight.

Reference figures for a standard 20ft dry container from one major line: about 33.2 cbm internal volume and a maximum payload of about 28 tonnes. In practice you load less than both. Hand-stacked bags and cartons rarely use all the space, and road weight limits at origin or destination often cap the gross weight well below the container rating. A 40ft gives roughly double the space but not double the weight.

Rough stowage factors (space per tonne, including gaps) help you plan:

Group Approx. cbm per tonne
Rice, sugar, pulses in bags 1.2–1.5
Flours in sacks 1.5–1.8
Oil in tins and cartons 1.3–1.5
Ground spices in cartons 2–3
Poha, papad, vermicelli, whole chilli 3–6

An illustrative 20ft resort mix might be 7 t rice, 3 t flours, 2.5 t pulses, 1.5 t sugar, 2.5 t oil and 1.2 t of spices and light dry goods. That is about 17.7 tonnes and roughly 27–28 cbm, so the box is full by space while well under its weight limit. If your order is heavy on rice and oil, you will hit weight first and can add light lines at almost no extra freight cost.

Load plan basics

  • Oils and liquids go low, never above flour or rice. A leaking tin ruins everything below it.
  • Strong-smelling items (asafoetida, cloves, dried fish if any) are sealed and kept away from rice and flour, which pick up odours.
  • Stack by item and outlet where possible so receiving can count quickly.
  • If you use pallets, they must be ISPM 15 heat-treated and marked.
  • Ask for dunnage and moisture absorbers. Containers sweat when they move from a cool hold into tropical heat.

How often to order

Order frequency for an island resort is set by four things: storeroom space, shelf life in your conditions, sailing frequency to your port, and cash tied up in stock.

A simple way to set the cycle:

  • Weeks of cover = shortest acceptable shelf life in store, or storeroom capacity, whichever is smaller.
  • Order quantity per line = weekly usage x (weeks of cover + transit and clearance weeks) + safety stock − stock on hand.

Many resorts settle on a container every 8 to 12 weeks for core dry goods. Flours and ground spices often drive the cycle because they lose quality fastest in heat and humidity. Rice, pulses, sugar and oil can carry more weeks of cover if you have the space.

Check the routing too. Some island ports are served by feeder vessels with transhipment at a regional hub, which adds days and an extra handling step. Plan safety stock around your real door-to-door time and the dates your buyer's agent can clear the cargo, and keep extra cover ahead of high season and festive menus.

Documents for a mixed container

  • Commercial invoice and packing list itemised by line, with HS code, net and gross weight, pack count and best-before date.
  • Phytosanitary certificate for the plant products in the shipment.
  • Fumigation certificate for sea cargo.
  • Certificate of origin if your market grants preferential duty or the importer needs it.
  • Any destination-specific labelling or import permit your agent requires. Check this before the order is packed, since relabelling on an island is slow.

Before you order

  • Usage data for at least six months, with peaks marked
  • Par levels and weeks of cover per line
  • Pack sizes agreed with the chef and the storekeeper
  • Weight and volume estimate for the container size you plan
  • Load plan rules: oils low, odorous goods separated
  • Document list confirmed with your clearing agent
